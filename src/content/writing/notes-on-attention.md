Every tool teaches a rhythm. Some encourage checking; others encourage staying. The distinction is easy to miss because both can look efficient during a five-minute demo.

## Measure the after-effect

Instead of asking only whether a tool is fast, notice the state it leaves you in. Can you remember why you opened it? Did the interface preserve the shape of the problem, or replace it with its own notifications and taxonomy?

A good tool lets attention pass through it. Its shortcuts become muscle memory, its organization remains understandable, and its defaults do not constantly ask to be managed.

## Fewer, deeper defaults

Customization is valuable when it removes repeated friction. Past that point it becomes a hobby adjacent to the work. A few strong defaults create more freedom than a wall of options because they allow practice to accumulate.

The tools I keep are rarely the most comprehensive. They are the ones that make it easiest to resume a thought.
